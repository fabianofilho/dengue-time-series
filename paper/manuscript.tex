\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Dengue imposes a recurrent and heavy burden on the
Brazilian health system, and municipal forecasts are used to plan vector control,
procurement and hospital capacity. Operational forecasting tools rely on models that
must be trained and periodically retrained for each city, which limits how widely they
can be deployed. Whether a pre-trained time-series foundation model can substitute for
that per-city pipeline has not, to our knowledge, been evaluated on Brazilian dengue data.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} We benchmarked seven forecasting models on monthly notified
dengue counts from InfoDengue for eight Brazilian state capitals covering January 2010
to December 2024 (178 monthly observations per city). Six models were trained per city
(SARIMAX, Ridge regression, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost and Random Forest, the last five
using recursive multi-step forecasting with 24 autoregressive lags), and one, TimesFM
2.5, was applied zero-shot with no city-specific training. Evaluation used rolling-origin
cross-validation with an expanding window (minimum training window 48 months, forecast
horizon 12 months, step 1 month), producing 119 folds and 1{,}428 pooled predictions per
model per city and 79{,}968 predictions overall. The pre-specified primary metric was
sMAPE; MAE and RMSE were reported alongside it. Uncertainty in the accuracy estimates was
quantified by bootstrap resampling of the pooled predictions (2{,}000 replicates,
percentile method). Split conformal prediction intervals, calibrated only on residuals
from earlier folds and stratified by horizon, were added at the 50\%, 80\% and 95\%
levels and assessed for empirical coverage and weighted interval score. Reporting follows
TRIPOD+AI.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} TimesFM achieved the lowest sMAPE in seven of eight cities
(mean across cities 74.6\%, range 55.6\% to 96.6\%) against 85.0\% for CatBoost, the best
purpose-trained model, 104.3\% for Ridge, 111.2\% for SARIMAX and 113.7\% for LightGBM. By
MAE it ranked first in seven of eight. The largest margin was in Rio de Janeiro (sMAPE
96.1\%, 95\% CI 93.3 to 98.7, versus 123.0\%, 120.1 to 126.0, for XGBoost). In S\~ao Paulo
the two leading models were closer and their intervals overlapped (75.5\%, 72.8 to 78.3,
versus 80.1\%, 77.3 to 82.9, for CatBoost). The three accuracy metrics selected the same
model in only four of eight cities, and in two of them, Belo Horizonte and Bras\'ilia, each
metric named a different winner. Conformal intervals were close to calibrated overall, at
93.0\% empirical coverage at the nominal 95\% level for TimesFM, but the ranking by
weighted interval score reversed between two equally defensible interval constructions, so
no probabilistic ranking is claimed. Combining the seven models did not beat the best
single model in seven of eight cities on the primary metric, although the stacked ensemble
achieved the lowest RMSE of any approach.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.} A zero-shot foundation model matched or exceeded
purpose-trained models for 12-month-ahead dengue point forecasting across heterogeneous
Brazilian capitals without any local training, which lowers the cost of extending
forecasting to municipalities that lack modelling capacity. That advantage does not
extend to probabilistic forecasts, where the ranking depends on the interval
construction, nor is it improved by combining models. Intervals were wide, and coverage
collapsed during the one epidemic in the series, so these forecasts are suited to planning
baselines rather than outbreak triggers.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} dengue; forecasting; foundation model; time series;
machine learning; Brazil; epidemiological surveillance
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dengue is the most widespread arboviral disease globally, and tropical Latin America
carries one of the heaviest burdens \citep{Leung2023}. Brazil reported the largest
epidemic in its recorded history in 2024, with outbreaks across all five macroregions
\citep{GurgelGoncalves2024}. The consequences reach beyond case counts: direct treatment
costs, lost productivity and crowding of primary care and emergency services concentrate
in exactly the regions with the least surge capacity \citep{Siqueira2022}.

Forecasting has an established place in this response. Reliable anticipation of case
volume allows health authorities to preposition supplies, time vector control and plan
hospital capacity before a peak \citep{Roster2022}. Brazil is unusually well equipped for
this: InfoDengue, operated jointly by Fiocruz and FGV, integrates notification records
from SINAN and publishes near-real-time municipal surveillance data
\citep{Codeco2018infodengue}. What the country lacks is an integrated forecasting layer
able to serve the diversity of local epidemic dynamics.

Comparative benchmarking of dengue forecasting in Brazil is no longer scarce. Recent work
has evaluated machine learning strategies for short-term forecasting across all 27
Brazilian capitals with climatic and socioeconomic covariates \citep{Silva2026capitals},
produced Bayesian probabilistic epidemic bands for 118 health districts
\citep{Freitas2025bands}, and run a prospective multi-team forecasting challenge across
five states \citep{Araujo2026sprint}. What remains open is narrower and specific.

First, that literature concentrates on short horizons and on models supplied with
exogenous covariates, so it cannot separate the contribution of the forecasting
architecture from the contribution of the extra information. Second, and more
consequential for deployment, the models that perform best are still trained city by
city, and the best model differs by city \citep{Chen2025rj,Fang2024}. A national
surveillance programme covering thousands of municipalities cannot maintain a bespoke
pipeline for each one. Third, a systematic review of dengue forecasting found that
studies rarely report calibration and seldom use comparable validation designs, which
makes quantitative synthesis across methods impossible \citep{Leung2023}; the reporting
of predictive uncertainty, as opposed to point accuracy, remains the weakest part of the
field.

Time-series foundation models offer a different arrangement. Pre-trained on large and
heterogeneous temporal corpora, they generate forecasts without any task-specific
training \citep{Das2024timesfm,Ansari2024chronos,Woo2024moirai}. Whether that paradigm
transfers to epidemic science has been posed as an open question but not settled
empirically for dengue \citep{Lau2026foundation}. If such a model performs
comparably to purpose-trained alternatives on dengue series, the marginal cost of
extending forecasting to an additional municipality falls to the cost of running
inference. Whether that transfers to dengue counts, which are volatile, strongly seasonal
and punctuated by epidemics an order of magnitude above baseline, has not to our knowledge
been evaluated. The recent Dengue Forecasting Sprint benchmarked six teams on Brazilian
dengue using machine learning and classical statistical approaches
\citep{Araujo2026sprint}; the entries are described at that level of generality, and we
did not identify a zero-shot foundation model among them.

This study therefore aimed to: (i) describe the evolution of dengue incidence across
eight Brazilian state capitals over 15 years; and (ii) benchmark a zero-shot foundation
model against six purpose-trained forecasting models under a single standardised
rolling-origin validation design.

\section{Methods}

Reporting follows the TRIPOD+AI statement for the development and evaluation of
prediction models using regression or machine learning methods
\citep{Collins2024tripod}. Analysis code, processed data and every result file underlying
the tables and figures are openly available at
\url{https://github.com/fabianofilho/dengue-time-series}.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

This study used exclusively publicly available aggregate surveillance records obtained
from the InfoDengue open application programming interface. The data are counts of
notified dengue cases per municipality per epidemiological week, contain no
individual-level information, and cannot be used to identify any person. Under Brazilian
National Health Council Resolution CNS 510/2016, Article 1, sole paragraph, such research
is not subject to registration or evaluation by the CEP/CONEP research ethics committee
system: item II covers research using publicly accessible information, and item V covers
research with databases whose information is aggregated with no possibility of individual
identification. Both apply here. Approval by an
Institutional Ethics Committee was therefore not required, and none was sought. No human
participants were recruited and no informed consent was applicable.

\subsection{Study setting and data source}

Eight Brazilian state capitals were studied: S\~ao Paulo (SP), Rio de Janeiro (RJ), Belo
Horizonte (MG), Bras\'ilia (DF), Fortaleza (CE), Recife (PE), Manaus (AM) and Salvador
(BA). Together they span the Southeast, Central-West, Northeast and North macroregions
and cover a wide range of climatic, demographic and transmission profiles.

Notification data were obtained through the InfoDengue public API
(\url{https://info.dengue.mat.br/}), which aggregates mandatory notifications from SINAN
\citep{Codeco2018infodengue}. Weekly counts for epidemiological week 1 of 2010 through
week 52 of 2024 were converted to calendar months by distributing each epidemiological
week uniformly across the seven days it covers and summing by month, yielding 178 complete
monthly observations per city and 1{,}424 city-months in total.

That conversion is not the obvious one, and the obvious one is wrong. Assigning each week
whole to the month containing its start date, which is what a naive monthly resample does,
produces a series in which some months receive four weeks of cases and others five. On
this dataset 118 of 180 months summed 28 days and 62 summed 35, a calendar-driven
oscillation of 25\% whose period of roughly 12.4 months does not even align with the
annual seasonality the models are trying to learn. The error is not small: in S\~ao Paulo
it displaced the median month by 10.1\%, reached 30.7\% at worst, and inflated the March
2024 peak by 16.8\%. Every result below uses the day-weighted conversion. Months only
partly covered by the weekly series are dropped, which removes January 2010, whose first
epidemiological week begins on the third, and December 2024, whose last week ends on the
twenty-eighth.

Reporting delay was assessed and found not to require correction: across all 782 weeks in
all eight cities the notified count and the InfoDengue nowcast estimate are identical,
indicating the nowcast has fully converged for this extraction.

Population denominators, used only to express burden as incidence in
Table~\ref{tab:descriptive} and not in any forecasting model, come from IBGE. We anchored
the annual series on the two actual enumerations, the 2010 and 2022 Censuses, and
interpolated geometrically between them at a constant annual rate, then extrapolated 2023
and 2024 at the same rate; monthly values were interpolated linearly from mid-year
anchors. We did not use the published intercensal estimate series, because it mixes two
incompatible regimes: values up to 2021 come from the pre-2022 projection, which the
Census revised downward by between 7.6\% (S\~ao Paulo) and 16.6\% (Salvador). Splicing
the two produces a discontinuity in 2022 in every city, and any rate-based analysis would
read that discontinuity as an epidemiological signal.

\subsection{Outcome and predictors}

The outcome was the monthly count of notified dengue cases per city, modelled on its
original scale with no transformation. Forecasts below zero were clipped to zero.

No exogenous covariates were used. This is a deliberate constraint: it holds the feature
set identical across all seven models, so that differences in accuracy are attributable to
the forecasting architecture rather than to unequal access to information. The six
purpose-trained models used 24 autoregressive lags of the monthly count, a two-year
lookback chosen to span the dominant inter-annual dengue cycle in Brazil. The
consequences of excluding climate covariates are addressed in the limitations.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{SARIMAX} served as the parametric statistical baseline, with non-seasonal order
$(1,1,1)$ and seasonal order $(1,1,0)_{12}$, estimated by maximum likelihood in
\texttt{statsmodels}. Orders were fixed across cities and folds rather than selected per
fold, which reflects realistic deployment but may understate its optimised performance.

\textbf{Ridge regression} provided a linear autoregressive baseline on the same 24 lags,
with the default L2 penalty. Unlike the tree ensembles, Ridge is not scale-invariant, and
the lags were standardised before fitting. This is not a cosmetic choice. Without
standardisation the penalty is negligible relative to counts in the thousands, and in the
earliest folds, where the number of training rows approaches the number of lags, the fit
becomes near-singular; recursive 12-step prediction then amplifies the result. In
Salvador a single fold, the one with 49 months of training, produced a forecast of
$8.7 \times 10^{14}$ cases and by itself dominated that city's pooled MAE and RMSE. After
standardisation no fold diverged in any city. We report this because the failure is
nearly silent under sMAPE, which is bounded and therefore absorbs a divergence of any
magnitude: the unscaled Salvador run returned a plausible-looking sMAPE of 96.3\% while
its MAE was $6.7 \times 10^{11}$.

\textbf{LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost and Random Forest}
\citep{Ke2017lightgbm,Chen2016xgboost,Prokhorenkova2018catboost,Breiman2001} were wrapped
in the recursive multi-step forecaster of \texttt{skforecast}
(\texttt{ForecasterRecursive}) with 24 lags. Each was refitted independently at every
fold on the data available at that point. Library defaults were used with a fixed random
seed; no hyperparameter search was performed, for the same reason the SARIMAX orders were
fixed.

\textbf{TimesFM 2.5} is a 200-million-parameter decoder-only transformer pre-trained by
Google DeepMind on a large multi-domain corpus of time series \citep{Das2024timesfm}. It
was loaded from the HuggingFace Hub (checkpoint
\texttt{google/timesfm-2.5-200m-pytorch}), with maximum context 512, maximum horizon 24,
input normalisation on and a positivity constraint. It was used strictly zero-shot: no
fine-tuning, no city-specific adaptation and no refitting at any fold. It therefore
receives strictly less information than its competitors, which see the city's own
training window at every fold.

One model specified in the study protocol was not evaluated and is reported here for
completeness. Prophet \citep{Taylor2018prophet} failed to run in seven of the eight
cities because of an unresolved dependency, and partial results from a single city are
not interpretable as a benchmark. It does not appear in the results and is not discussed
as though it had been tested.

\subsection{Evaluation design}

We used rolling-origin cross-validation with an expanding training window
\citep{Tashman2000,Hewamalage2023}. At each fold the model is fitted only on observations
preceding the forecast origin and predicts the following 12 months, so no information
from the evaluation period reaches the model \citep{Kapoor2023}. The minimum training
window was 48 months, chosen so that every supervised model sees at least two complete
annual cycles before its first forecast; the origin then advances one month at a time.

For a series of length $T = 178$ with minimum training size $m = 48$ and horizon
$h = 12$, this yields $T - m - h + 1 = 119$ folds per city, $119 \times 12 = 1{,}428$
predictions per model per city, and $1{,}428 \times 7 \times 8 = 79{,}968$ predictions in
total.

This is temporal out-of-sample evaluation within a single data source, which is internal
validation under the TRIPOD+AI nomenclature. It is not external validation: no
independent cohort, surveillance system or country was used. The eight cities are eight
separate internal validations, not a geographic external validation, because each
purpose-trained model is fitted on the same city it is evaluated in.

\subsection{Performance metrics and uncertainty}

The primary metric, declared before analysis, was sMAPE, computed on predictions pooled
across all folds within a city:
\[
\mathrm{sMAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}
\frac{|y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{(|y_t| + |\hat{y}_t|)/2}.
\]
sMAPE is bounded and treats over- and under-forecasting symmetrically, which suits series
that spend most months near a low baseline and occasionally spike
\citep{Hewamalage2023}. MAE and RMSE were reported alongside it on the original case
scale. The three are reported together because they reward different behaviour: sMAPE
rewards accuracy in inter-epidemic troughs, RMSE rewards accuracy at peaks, and reporting
only one would conceal the disagreement quantified in Table~\ref{tab:metric-disagreement}.

Uncertainty was quantified by resampling the 1{,}428 paired predictions with replacement
(2{,}000 replicates, percentile method) to obtain 95\% intervals for sMAPE. Predictions
from the same rolling-origin fold, and from adjacent origins, are not independent;
these intervals therefore understate the true uncertainty and are interpreted throughout
as a lower bound on the spread rather than as a formal test. No hypothesis test of
model superiority was performed, and none is claimed.

\subsection{Prediction intervals}

Point forecasts alone cannot tell a health authority how much confidence to place in a
number, and TRIPOD+AI requires that predictive uncertainty be reported and assessed
\citep{Collins2024tripod}. We therefore constructed prediction intervals around every
point forecast already produced, without refitting any model, using split conformal
prediction \citep{Angelopoulos2021conformal}.

For a given model, city and horizon step $h$, the interval at fold $k$ is calibrated on
the residuals that same model produced at the same $h$ in folds strictly earlier than
$k$. Restricting calibration to earlier folds preserves the same no-leakage property the
point forecasts have: nothing from the evaluated period, or from any later period,
informs the interval. Quantiles use the finite-sample conformal correction, the
$\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$-th order statistic of $n$ calibration residuals, which
without correction leaves coverage systematically below nominal at small $n$. We required
20 prior folds before emitting an interval, so the first 20 folds of each series carry
none and 1{,}188 of the 1{,}428 predictions per model per city are evaluated. Intervals
were produced at the 50\%, 80\% and 95\% nominal levels, and stratified by $h$ because
forecast uncertainty grows with the horizon.

The scale on which residuals are calibrated is a consequential choice, and we could not
find a basis for preferring one, so both are reported throughout. Additive intervals
calibrate raw residuals and give a constant width within a horizon, which is too wide in
the inter-epidemic trough and too narrow at the peak. Multiplicative intervals calibrate
residuals of $\log(1+y)$ and scale with the predicted level, which suits a series
spanning four orders of magnitude but couples the models' errors: a model that
occasionally predicts near zero while thousands of cases occur generates residuals of
$\log(\text{thousands})$, and that quantile widens the intervals of every other
prediction at the same horizon. TimesFM does exactly this, returning a forecast of zero
in 101 of 1{,}428 predictions in Belo Horizonte and 30 in Rio de Janeiro, against none and
one for CatBoost. We report both constructions and claim only what holds under both.

Intervals were assessed on three axes: empirical coverage against the nominal level,
which is the calibration assessment; interval width, which is sharpness; and the weighted
interval score (WIS), a proper scoring rule that combines the two into a single value on
the outcome scale \citep{Bracher2021wis,Gneiting2007scoring}. Because WIS is
scale-dependent and these cities differ by more than an order of magnitude in case
volume, we divide each city's WIS by that city's mean observed count before averaging
across cities, and report this as rWIS.

\subsection{Model combinations}

Because no single model wins under every metric, we tested whether combining them helps.
Four combinations of the seven models were evaluated on the forecasts already produced,
without refitting anything: the arithmetic mean, the median, a mean weighted by the
inverse of each model's mean absolute error, and a stacked ensemble using standardised
Ridge regression on the members' predictions.

Two design choices decide whether such a comparison means anything. First, weights and the
stacking meta-model are fitted only on folds strictly earlier than the one being
predicted, on the same expanding window used for the point forecasts and the conformal
intervals. Selecting weights on the whole backtest makes a combination look better than it
is and leaves no trace in the reported number. Second, individual models and combinations
are scored on exactly the same rows: the first 20 folds are withheld as a training window
for the combiners, so 1{,}188 of the 1{,}428 predictions per approach per city are
evaluated. Comparing a combination scored on 99 folds against a single model scored on 119
would favour the combination, because the withheld folds are those with the shortest
training window and therefore the hardest. All seven models enter as members, including
the weak ones, since discarding a member on the basis of the same backtest used to
evaluate the result would be selection on the outcome.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Descriptive characteristics}

Table~\ref{tab:descriptive} summarises the eight series and Figure~\ref{fig:series} shows
them. In absolute counts the cities differ by more than an order of magnitude: S\~ao
Paulo accumulated 1{,}702{,}694 notified cases over the period against 116{,}190 in
Salvador. Standardising by population reorders them substantially. Belo Horizonte carries
the highest burden at a mean 3{,}124 notified cases per 100{,}000 inhabitants per year,
followed by Bras\'ilia at 1{,}740; S\~ao Paulo, first by raw count, falls to fourth at
1{,}002, and Salvador is lowest at 312. The gap between the highest and lowest incidence
is tenfold, against a roughly fifteenfold gap in raw counts driven largely by city size.

The series are also extremely volatile, and this matters more for forecasting than the
level does. The coefficient of variation of the monthly count ranges from 108\% in
Salvador to 432\% in S\~ao Paulo, where the median month recorded 1{,}192 cases and the
peak month 342{,}838, a ratio of about 288 to 1. Manaus shows the same pattern at a
smaller scale (median 274, maximum 19{,}556). Median monthly incidence is between 10 and
43 per 100{,}000 in every city, while peak monthly incidence reaches 4{,}938 in Belo
Horizonte, so the burden is concentrated in a handful of months rather than spread across
the series. That concentration is the central difficulty for every model evaluated here,
and it is what separates the metrics in Section~\ref{sec:disagreement}.

\subsection{Forecasting accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:smape} reports sMAPE with bootstrap intervals and
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} displays the same values as a heatmap. TimesFM achieved the
lowest sMAPE in seven of eight cities, with a mean across cities of 75.3\% (range 56.0\%
in Manaus to 97.1\% in Belo Horizonte). The best purpose-trained model on the same
summary was CatBoost at 85.0\%, followed by XGBoost at 86.7\%, Random Forest at 88.9\%,
Ridge at 104.3\%, SARIMAX at 111.2\% and LightGBM at 113.7\%.

The margin varies sharply by city and the intervals matter for reading it. In Rio de
Janeiro the separation is wide and the intervals do not overlap: TimesFM 96.1\% (95\% CI
93.3 to 98.7) against 123.0\% (120.1 to 126.0) for XGBoost, the best supervised model
there. Manaus, Fortaleza and Recife show the same pattern of non-overlap. In S\~ao Paulo
the two leading models are closer and their intervals overlap, at 75.5\% (72.8 to 78.3)
for TimesFM against 80.1\% (77.3 to 82.9) for CatBoost, so that gap is not treated as
established. Belo Horizonte is the one city where a supervised model leads on the primary
metric, with XGBoost at 87.5\% (84.1 to 90.7) against 96.6\% (93.2 to 99.7) for TimesFM.

\subsection{Absolute error}

Table~\ref{tab:mae-rmse} reports MAE and RMSE on the case-count scale. By MAE, TimesFM
ranked first in seven of eight cities, including Belo Horizonte, where it led on sMAPE in
none; CatBoost took Bras\'ilia. The RMSE ranking is far less consistent: TimesFM led in
four cities, LightGBM in two and Ridge in two, despite LightGBM and Ridge ranking last and
second-to-last on the primary metric overall. A model can win on RMSE by being
conservative at epidemic peaks while being poor everywhere else, and that is what happens
here.

The gap between MAE and RMSE within a city quantifies how much of the total error sits in
a few months. In S\~ao Paulo the best MAE is 8{,}759 cases against an RMSE above 41{,}000
for every model, a ratio consistent with error concentrated almost entirely in epidemic
peaks rather than spread across the series.

\subsection{The metrics disagree}
\label{sec:disagreement}

Table~\ref{tab:metric-disagreement} and Figure~\ref{fig:disagreement} make explicit a
result that a single-metric report would hide: sMAPE, MAE and RMSE select the same model
in only four of the eight cities. Belo Horizonte and Bras\'ilia are the extreme cases:
in each, all three metrics name a different winner. In Belo Horizonte XGBoost wins on
sMAPE, and the headline claim of this study fails there; TimesFM wins on MAE; and Ridge
wins on RMSE despite having one of the worst sMAPE values in that city. In Bras\'ilia the
winners are TimesFM, CatBoost and LightGBM respectively. Every one of those statements is
true, and which is reported determines whether either city reads as a counterexample.

The mechanism is arithmetic rather than epidemiological. sMAPE normalises each error by
the magnitude of the observation, so the many low-count months between epidemics dominate
it. RMSE squares the error, so the few epidemic months dominate instead. A model that
tracks the baseline well and misses peaks scores well on sMAPE and badly on RMSE, and the
reverse also holds. We therefore report the primary metric as declared in advance and
present the other two alongside rather than selecting the metric that favours a
conclusion.

\subsection{Prediction intervals are calibrated, except when it matters}
\label{sec:intervals-fail}

Table~\ref{tab:intervals} reports coverage, sharpness and rWIS for both interval
constructions, and Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} shows the same information graphically.
Conformal calibration works: pooled over all folds and cities, empirical coverage tracks
the nominal level closely for every model except LightGBM. At the nominal 95\% level
TimesFM achieved 91.9\% under the multiplicative construction and 93.0\% under the
additive one, and CatBoost 91.0\% and 91.4\%. LightGBM was the worst calibrated in both,
at 89.4\% and 87.3\%. Ridge and SARIMAX were the only models to reach or exceed nominal
coverage, at 95.8\% and 94.2\% under the multiplicative construction, and they do so for
an unflattering reason: their point forecasts are poor enough that the calibration
residuals are very wide, which buys coverage with uninformative intervals.

Coverage degrades mildly with the horizon, from 95.2\% at one month ahead to 91.5\% at
twelve under the multiplicative construction, and from 95.8\% to 91.7\% under the
additive one. This is the expected and benign pattern.

The failure that matters is temporal rather than structural. Restricted to the years
before 2024, coverage at the nominal 95\% level is 92.8\% (multiplicative) and 93.6\%
(additive). In 2024 it falls to 87.2\% and 69.2\% respectively. Figure~\ref{fig:fan}
shows the mechanism for S\~ao Paulo: through a decade of ordinary years the observed
series sits inside the band, and the 2024 epidemic exits it entirely for several
consecutive months, peaking above 340{,}000 cases against an upper bound an order of
magnitude below. The intervals are honest about ordinary variation and silent about the event a
surveillance system most needs warning of.

Sharpness is poor throughout, and this qualifies the coverage result. The median width of
the 95\% interval is 13.0 times the median observed count under the additive construction
and 25.8 times under the multiplicative one, reaching 60.7 and 416.2 times in the worst
city. An interval that spans an order of magnitude around the forecast is calibrated in
the technical sense and close to useless for planning.

\subsection{The probabilistic ranking is not robust}

Under the multiplicative construction, CatBoost has the lowest rWIS at 0.68 while TimesFM
ranks fourth at 1.37. Under the additive construction the ordering reverses: TimesFM has
the lowest rWIS at 0.58 and CatBoost drops to third at 0.67. Both constructions are
defensible, both are calibrated to roughly the same degree, and they disagree about which
model produces the better probabilistic forecast.

The reason is the coupling described in the Methods. TimesFM produces the better point
forecast in Belo Horizonte and Rio de Janeiro by MAE, but it also returns exact zeros in
those two cities, which inflates its multiplicative intervals everywhere and costs it the
comparison on that scale.

We therefore report no probabilistic ranking of the models. The point-forecast ranking in
Sections~3.2 and 3.3 is stable across sMAPE and MAE; the probabilistic ranking is an
artefact of a modelling choice that the data do not settle.

\subsection{Combining models does not rescue the ranking}
\label{sec:ensembles}

Table~\ref{tab:ensembles} reports the four combinations alongside the seven individual
models on the same evaluation set, and Figure~\ref{fig:ensembles} shows the per-city
outcome. The best combination fails to beat the best single model of that city in seven of
eight cities. The single exception is S\~ao Paulo, where the median ensemble improves on
the best member by 1.3 sMAPE points. Elsewhere the shortfall reaches 12.7 points in Rio de
Janeiro and 12.5 in Fortaleza.

Among the combinations themselves the ordering is informative. The median ensemble is the
best at 79.0\% mean sMAPE, ahead of every individual model except TimesFM, and its
advantage over the arithmetic mean at 87.6\% comes from resisting the members that
diverge: LightGBM and SARIMAX drag an average but not a median. Stacking is the worst
combination on the primary metric at 94.8\%.

The metric dependence documented in Section~\ref{sec:disagreement} reappears here, and it
reverses the conclusion. On sMAPE and MAE the best approach overall is a single model,
TimesFM. On RMSE the best approach overall is the stacked ensemble, at 10{,}532 against
10{,}862 for TimesFM, with the weighted ensemble second. Stacking is simultaneously the
worst combination at tracking the inter-epidemic baseline and the best at not being
catastrophically wrong at peaks. Whether combining models helps therefore has no
metric-free answer in these data.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Main findings}

Across eight Brazilian state capitals and 15 years of surveillance data, a foundation
model applied with no local training matched or exceeded six purpose-trained models at a
12-month horizon. It ranked first by sMAPE and by MAE in seven of eight cities, losing
Belo Horizonte on the former and Bras\'ilia on the latter. The comparison is asymmetric in TimesFM's disfavour: the supervised models were
refitted on each city at each of 119 folds, while TimesFM saw the series only as context
at inference.

The operational consequence is about cost rather than accuracy. The best supervised model
is not the same model in every city: four different ones take that place across the eight
capitals, CatBoost in three, XGBoost in three, Ridge in Manaus and Random Forest in
Salvador (Figure~\ref{fig:vsbest}). A per-city pipeline therefore requires per-city model
selection, retraining and monitoring, and the selection itself consumes data that could
otherwise be used for fitting. A single unmodified foundation model removes that
overhead, which is what makes extension to municipalities without modelling capacity
plausible.

That said, the accuracy in absolute terms is poor everywhere. A mean sMAPE of 75\% is not
a small error, and no model anticipated the 2024 epidemic in any city. The honest
reading is that these models provide a reasonable baseline expectation for planning under
ordinary conditions and are not outbreak detectors.

\subsection{Comparison with the literature}

Published Brazilian benchmarks have largely compared ARIMA variants against tree ensembles
over short horizons and often a single city
\citep{Roster2022,Chen2025rj,Sebastianelli2024}. Their consistent finding, that
tree-based models beat classical parametric ones on volatile dengue series
\citep{Fang2024}, is reproduced here: every tree ensemble except LightGBM outperformed
SARIMAX on the primary metric. Our contribution is to show that the additional step from
purpose-trained ensembles to a zero-shot foundation model is achievable without
sacrificing accuracy. The magnitude of the gain over SARIMAX, roughly 30 sMAPE points on
average, is of the same order as the gains that dynamic ensemble approaches have reported
against fixed parametric models on Brazilian dengue data \citep{McGough2021}.

Neural approaches to dengue forecasting, including LSTM architectures applied across
Brazil, need substantial per-city history and degrade under distribution shift
\citep{Chen2025lstm}. Zero-shot inference sidesteps both problems by construction,
though it introduces a different dependency: performance rests on how well the
pre-training corpus covers epidemic-like dynamics, which is not inspectable.

The most directly comparable work is the 2024 Dengue Forecasting Sprint, in which six
international teams produced probabilistic forecasts for five Brazilian states using
machine learning and classical statistical models with climate covariates
\citep{Araujo2026sprint}. Three differences bound what the present study adds. Their
design is prospective, ours retrospective, and a prospective challenge is the stronger
evidence for the question of which model to deploy; we make no claim to have tested
models under real forecasting conditions. Their unit is the state and ours the
municipality, which matters because the per-city pipeline burden that motivates a
zero-shot model scales with the number of units, and Brazil has 27 states against 5{,}570
municipalities. And they supplied climate covariates while we deliberately withheld them,
so the two studies answer different questions: theirs how well dengue can be forecast
with the information available, ours how forecasting architectures compare when the
information is held equal.

Where the two studies overlap they agree, and the agreement is worth stating plainly
because it concerns our least comfortable findings. The Sprint concluded that no single
model consistently excelled, particularly during the unprecedented 2024 season, and that
ensembles were needed to obtain stable performance. We reach the same place from a
different direction: the model ranking here depends on which accuracy metric is chosen in
half the cities (Section~\ref{sec:disagreement}), the probabilistic ranking reverses
between two defensible interval constructions, and interval coverage collapses in 2024.
Two independent evaluations of Brazilian dengue forecasting, one prospective with
covariates at state level and one retrospective without covariates at municipal level,
both find that model choice is unstable and that 2024 defeated every approach. That
convergence is a stronger result than either study alone, and it argues against
single-model deployment more forcefully than our benchmark does on its own.

Two further studies bound the contribution and are worth naming precisely, because each
covers ground we do not. \citet{Silva2026capitals} evaluated machine learning strategies
for weekly dengue forecasting across all 27 Brazilian capitals at horizons up to four
weeks, with climatic and socioeconomic covariates. Their coverage of the country is
wider than ours and their design is the natural comparator for short-term operational
warning. The present study addresses a different decision: a 12-month horizon serves
annual planning rather than outbreak response, and withholding covariates isolates the
architecture, which is what makes a zero-shot model comparable to a trained one on equal
terms. Neither study substitutes for the other, and a reader choosing a model for
four-week warning should prefer theirs.

\citet{Freitas2025bands} produced Bayesian probabilistic epidemic bands for 118 Brazilian
health districts 52 weeks ahead, at a spatial resolution far finer than ours and already
oriented to Ministry of Health use. That work and ours approach predictive uncertainty
from opposite directions: they derive bands from the posterior of a single specified
model, while we wrap distribution-free intervals around the point forecasts of seven
different models and then measure whether the stated coverage holds. The complementarity
is the useful part. A calibration audit of the kind reported in
Section~\ref{sec:intervals-fail} has not, to our knowledge, been applied to their bands,
and our finding that coverage survives a decade of ordinary years and then fails in 2024
is a question their framework should also be asked.

A second study overlaps more narrowly but more sharply. Bannoud and colleagues forecast
dengue in S\~ao Paulo with fourteen machine learning algorithms, urban incident predictors
and a virtual data augmentation scheme, training on 2014 to 2022 and testing on 2023
through mid-2025 \citep{Bannoud2026augment}. Their best performers, AdaBoost and CatBoost,
place a gradient boosting method at the top in the same city where CatBoost is our best
purpose-trained model, so the two studies agree on the supervised ranking where they can
be compared.

They differ in what each was built to answer. Theirs is one city with informative
covariates and a single chronological split; ours is eight cities with no covariates and
119 rolling origins, which trades depth in one setting for a repeated estimate across
several. Neither design dominates: a single split yields one realisation of the test
period and cannot separate model skill from the particular months it happened to cover,
while a rolling origin cannot tell how a model would behave with information it was never
given.

The more important difference runs the other way, and it is a limitation of the present
work rather than of theirs. We document that every model failed in 2024 and that interval
coverage collapsed there, and we stop at documenting it. They target exactly that failure,
reporting that augmenting the training data with synthetic extremes reduced error
substantially when test values exceeded the training maximum. That is a candidate remedy
for the central weakness we identify and did not attempt, and it should be evaluated
against the multi-city, zero-shot setting benchmarked here before any of these models is
considered for prospective use. We note that their abstract does not report prediction
intervals, so whether augmentation repairs calibration during an epidemic, as opposed to
point accuracy, remains open.

Where the two diverge is on the remedy. The Sprint found that aggregation delivered the
stability no single entry achieved, and the resulting ensembles were adopted by Brazilian
health authorities. We tested four combinations and found that none beats the best
individual model in seven of eight cities on the primary metric
(Section~\ref{sec:ensembles}). The difference is unlikely to be a contradiction. Their
members were six independent teams with different feature sets and climate covariates;
ours are seven models sharing one lag structure and no covariates at all. Aggregation
rewards disagreement between members, and by construction we removed most of it. The
practical reading is that an ensemble is worth building for dengue surveillance in Brazil,
but that assembling one out of variations on a single autoregressive recipe will not
deliver the benefit.

The 12-month horizon here is considerably longer than the 4 to 8 weeks typical of
published dengue benchmarks. That makes these results relevant to annual planning cycles
and correspondingly less relevant to short-term outbreak response, and it also makes the
error magnitudes not directly comparable to the short-horizon literature.

\subsection{Belo Horizonte}

Belo Horizonte is the one city where a supervised model leads on the primary metric, and
it is worth being precise about what that means. XGBoost leads on sMAPE, TimesFM on MAE,
and Ridge on RMSE. The city's series combines sharp high-magnitude peaks with long near-zero
troughs, a profile that lag features capture well and that penalises a model normalising
error by observation magnitude. Cities with this signature are candidates for a hybrid
arrangement in which foundation model output is corrected by a locally fitted residual
model, but that design was not tested here and we make no claim about its performance.

\subsection{Limitations}

Nine limitations bound these conclusions.

First, the outcome is notified cases, which under-represent true infections
\citep{Siqueira2022}. What is forecast is the behaviour of the surveillance system,
including reporting delay and saturation during peaks, not biological incidence.

Second, no exogenous covariates were used. Models with access to rainfall, temperature
and vector indices outperform univariate approaches for outbreak onset
\citep{Barcellos2024,Fang2024}, so the TimesFM advantage may narrow or reverse once
supervised models receive informative features that a zero-shot model cannot consume.

Third, the prediction intervals are calibrated but not sharp, and their calibration is
conditional in a way that matters operationally. Median 95\% interval width is between
13.5 and 27.9 times the median observed count depending on construction, and coverage
falls to 71.3\% in 2024. An interval that is correct on average across a decade while
failing during the one epidemic in the series does not support the decision it would be
used for. Adaptive conformal methods that update the calibration set under distribution
shift \citep{Gibbs2021adaptive} are the natural next step and were not attempted here.

Fourth, the bootstrap intervals resample predictions that are serially dependent within
and across folds. They understate uncertainty and are reported as a lower bound, not as a
significance test. Where intervals overlap, as in S\~ao Paulo, no advantage is claimed.

Fifth, validation is internal and temporal. No independent surveillance system,
country or held-out cohort was used, and the eight cities constitute eight internal
validations rather than a geographic external validation.

Sixth, SARIMAX orders and all tree hyperparameters were fixed rather than tuned. This
mirrors low-maintenance deployment but understates what a carefully tuned supervised
pipeline could achieve, which makes the comparison conservative in TimesFM's favour on
maintenance cost and potentially generous on accuracy.

Seventh, one TimesFM checkpoint was evaluated on eight capitals. Whether the result holds
for other foundation models \citep{Ansari2024chronos,Woo2024moirai}, other checkpoints,
or the smaller municipalities with shorter and more irregular reporting histories that
make up most of Brazil, is untested.

Eighth, we did not attempt any remedy for the distribution shift that defeated every
model in 2024. Training-set augmentation with synthetic extremes has been reported to cut
error substantially in exactly this situation for S\~ao Paulo
\citep{Bannoud2026augment}, and adaptive conformal methods \citep{Gibbs2021adaptive}
address the interval side of the same problem. Both are untested here, and a benchmark
that identifies a failure without testing the available fixes for it is incomplete.

Ninth, the combinations tested here are unweighted or linear in the members'
predictions, and all members share the same 24 lagged inputs. That shared input is the
most likely reason combining did not help: models trained on identical features capture
similar structure, so a combination averages correlated errors rather than adding
information. The Dengue Forecasting Sprint combined six independent teams with different
feature sets and climate covariates \citep{Araujo2026sprint}, which is a far more
favourable setting for aggregation than ours. Our negative result is therefore about
combining redundant models, not about ensembles in general, and it does not contradict
theirs.

\subsection{Conclusion}

A zero-shot foundation model matched or exceeded six purpose-trained models for
12-month-ahead dengue point forecasting in eight Brazilian state capitals, without any
city-specific training, and did so under a validation design that gave its competitors
repeated access to local data it never saw. This supports evaluating foundation models as
a low-maintenance forecasting layer within InfoDengue, particularly for municipalities
where maintaining a bespoke model is not feasible.

Two results bound that recommendation. The advantage does not carry over to probabilistic
forecasts: the ranking by weighted interval score reverses between two equally defensible
interval constructions, so the choice of model cannot be settled on that basis with these
data. And the intervals, while calibrated across the study period as a whole, are wide
and lose coverage precisely during the 2024 epidemic. Neither finding argues against
deploying these forecasts as a planning baseline; both argue against reading them as
outbreak warnings.
